MartisDux · concept in development

Make Mars feel navigable.

MartisDux is an open-source concept for reading the Martian surface: terrain, routes, scientific sites and hazards on one layered map — and, one day, in augmented reality on the table in front of you.

Why we started

An unfamiliar world deserves a clearer map.

Mars has been photographed, measured and mapped from orbit for decades. Yet a simple question about one place — how steep is that slope, what is that rock, can I get there from here — still means digging through archives that were never meant to be read together.

MartisDux is our attempt to put those answers in one view. Pick a place and see its shape, the features worth visiting, a route across it and the hazards along the way, all drawn over the same ground.

Three things that make a map matter more on Mars

  1. No satellite navigation

    There is no GPS-style constellation around Mars. Knowing where you are, and what lies ahead, depends on maps built from orbital data and on landmarks you can recognise.

  2. Minutes of delay

    A radio signal takes roughly 3 to 22 minutes to cross between Earth and Mars, depending on where the planets are. Explorers will need answers close at hand, not a conversation away.

  3. Knowledge in many places

    Orbiters, landers and rovers have each studied Mars with their own instruments, resolutions and archives. Reading them side by side is still hard work.

From planetary data to a clearer picture.

Intended experience · not yet built

From the whole planet down to a single walk across it, in three steps.

  1. Step 1

    Choose a place

    Start from the whole planet and zoom in on somewhere specific: a landing site, a crater floor, the edge of a canyon.

  2. Step 2

    Read it in layers

    Turn on what you need — elevation and slope, sites of scientific interest, hazards — and see how they relate on the same ground.

  3. Step 3

    Study a route

    Sketch a path to a destination and see what it crosses. The map informs a person’s judgement; it doesn’t plan the walk for them.

What makes this hard

Different scales
Global elevation from laser altimetry (MOLA) is gridded at hundreds of metres per pixel. Stereo models from the HiRISE camera reach about a metre. Combining them without inventing detail is the first problem.
Patchy coverage
The sharpest images cover only small parts of the planet, so what we know changes from place to place. The map has to show where it knows less.
Coordinates that disagree
Datasets use different map projections, and Mars longitudes can run east-positive or west-positive. Making one point land in the same spot on every layer takes care.
Credit and limits
Every layer comes from a mission team. Each needs proper citation, and a note on what it can’t tell you.

A route, read against the land it crosses.

A path is more than a line between two points. Drawn over the terrain, it shows what it passes: rising ground, loose material, shadowed slopes. The goal is to help people study a journey, not to plan one automatically.

Where the planet’s history is exposed.

Layered outcrops, old river channels, crater walls and mineral signatures are where much of Mars’s past is recorded. Marking them turns a landscape into destinations, each with a short note on why it matters.

Conditions that shape when, and whether, to go.

Steep or rough ground, loose sand, lighting and dust all affect where it makes sense to travel. Where reliable data exists, this layer would put it next to the route — with a note on its limits.

Open source

Exploration is better when knowledge is shared.

The data behind a map like this was gathered by public missions. The tools that help people read it should be open too.

We intend to develop MartisDux in the open — code, methods and data sources — so anyone can check how a map was made, fix what’s wrong with it, or build something of their own on top.

  1. Transparency

    Every layer should say where its data came from, how it was processed and what it can’t tell you.

  2. Reproducibility

    Open code and documented steps mean anyone can check a result, rebuild a map or find a mistake.

  3. Collaboration

    Scientists, developers, designers and teachers each notice different problems. Openness lets all of them help.

  4. Learning

    Students should be able to look inside the map, not only at it.

Before anyone walks on Mars, someone has to read the ground.

MartisDux is our attempt to make that reading easier — first on a map, then in the room with you.
